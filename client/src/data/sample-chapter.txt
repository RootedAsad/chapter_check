Chapter Seven: The Lighthouse Keeper

Mr. Hale had kept the light at Garrow Point for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had never once seen the harbor so quiet. The gulls were gone. The fishing boats sat low and still at their moorings. Even the wind, which usually shoved at the shutters like an impatient visitor, had dropped away to nothing.

"Something's wrong," Mara said. She stood at the window with her arms wrapped around herself. "It's too calm."

"Calm is not a crime," he answered, though he did not sound convinced.

Dr. Reyes arrived at 5 p.m. with a leather bag, a damp coat, and the unmistakable look of a man who had walked further than he had intended. He set the bag on the kitchen table and, without waiting to be asked, began to explain that the barometric measurements he had taken throughout the afternoon indicated an extraordinarily rapid deterioration in atmospheric pressure, which, notwithstanding the apparent tranquility of the surrounding waters, strongly suggested that an unprecedented meteorological disturbance was approaching the coastline faster than the regional forecasting authorities had been willing to acknowledge.

Mara stared at him. "In English, please."

"A storm is coming. A big one." He rubbed his eyes. "Tonight."

Mr. Hale said nothing for a long moment. Then he took his oilskin from its hook, checked the lamp oil twice, and began the long climb up the spiral stairs, counting each iron step the way he had counted them every evening since his wife had died.

At the top, the lens waited in the dark like a great glass eye. He lit the lamp. The beam swung out across the water, pale and steady, and for a few seconds the whole bay seemed to hold its breath.

Then the first wave hit the rocks below.

It did not sound like water. It sounded like a door slamming in an enormous empty house. Mara cried out from the stairwell, and Mr. Hale, who had spent three decades preparing for precisely this night and who now found that no amount of preparation could quiet the hammering of his own heart, gripped the brass rail with both hands and understood that the sea, having waited patiently for generations of careless sailors and complacent keepers alike, had finally decided to collect everything it was owed.

"Hold the lamp steady!" he shouted. "Whatever happens, we do not let that light go out."

Dr. Reyes appeared beside her, soaked to the skin. "How long can it last?"

"As long as it has to," said Mr. Hale.

Outside, the rain began.
